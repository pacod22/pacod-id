# Threat model

This is pacod-id's threat model, checked against the code as it is built
(2026-10-12, stage 10). Each row says what protects against a threat, where that lives, and what
risk remains. Where the code is weaker than its design or than good practice, the remaining
risk starts with **Open:** (to be fixed, with the stage that fixes it) or **Accepted:** (a
deliberate choice, with the reason). A review of stage 10 found the weaknesses this document
first listed; those fixed then are described as controls, with the test that holds them.

Paths without a directory are in `crates/pacod-id/src/`. Line numbers are those of the date
above and move with the code; the function names do not.

## What is worth protecting

- **Accounts.** Password hashes, Google links, SSO sessions and grants. Whoever takes over an
  account acts as that person in every product of the installation (one account pool: a
  person's `sub` is the same in every realm).
- **The signing keys.** Whoever has one signs tokens every product accepts, for anyone.
- **Tokens in circulation.** Refresh tokens, service tokens (`pidt_`, imported `pgwa_`), SSO
  cookies, authorization codes, and the one-time links in mails (reset, verification,
  invitation, address change).
- **Role bindings and the feed.** Every product decides from its copy of them; a forged binding
  or a lost revocation is wrong access everywhere.
- **Personal data.** Addresses, names, client addresses and user agents of sessions, and the
  identity audit log.
- **Sign-in itself.** pacod-id is the one place where sign-in can fail for every product.

## Who and what is trusted

| Party | Trusted for |
|---|---|
| People in a browser | Nothing beyond their own account. Their writes to `/ui-api` must come from the realm's own pages. |
| A product's BFF (confidential client) | Its own sign-ins and refresh tokens; ending sessions it had a grant in. |
| A public client (a native app) | Nothing: PKCE alone protects its codes, rotation its refresh tokens. |
| A product's service (service client) | Its own product's manifest, scopes and feed; acting for the people whose tokens it holds, within their rights; ending any session; introspecting tokens. |
| Holders of `id.admin` and other roles | What their bindings allow through the admin API or the hosted admin pages. |
| Google (one client per realm) | The identity it asserts: `sub`, the address and `email_verified`. |
| The reverse proxy in front | TLS, and the client address in `X-Forwarded-For` when it is listed in `PID_TRUSTED_PROXIES`. It sees every request line. |
| The network between products and the internal listener | The authenticity of that traffic: it is plain HTTP. |
| The SMTP relay and the mailboxes | Delivering one-time links. Whoever reads them can use them. |
| PostgreSQL | Storage. It holds password hashes, SHA-256 or HMAC of every token, and signing keys sealed under the key-encryption key, never the key-encryption key itself. |
| Operators of the host or cluster | Everything. They hold `PID_KEK`, the peppers, the configuration and the database. |

## Threats and controls

### Credentials and sign-in

| Threat | Control as built | Where in the code | Remaining risk |
|---|---|---|---|
| Guessing passwords online | Argon2id with m = 19,456 KiB, t = 2, p = 1; `PID_ARGON2_M_KIB` can raise the memory, never lower it; a weaker or bcrypt hash is replaced at the next successful sign-in. At most 2 to 16 hashes run at once. Counters live in PostgreSQL, shared by every instance: 10 attempts in 15 minutes per account from browsers it has not used, a separate 10 per known browser and account, 30 per IPv4 address or IPv6 /64, each locking for 15 minutes. Every attempt takes its slot atomically before the password is hashed, so a burst of parallel guesses gets no more tries than the limit; a successful sign-in gives its slots back. The same holds for re-entering the password on the account pages. Unknown accounts and accounts without a password are checked against a dummy hash, and every failure gets the same 401. | `crypto/password.rs:11-71`, `oidc/flows.rs` (`password_step`), `store/throttle.rs` (`throttle_take`), `me.rs` (`check_password`), `oidc/mod.rs` (`ip_bucket`); test `a_burst_of_parallel_guesses_gets_no_more_tries_than_the_limit` | Guessing spread over many addresses stays below the address limit; a realm may allow 8 characters; MFA comes in phase 4. |
| Weak passwords | A realm's minimum (12 by default; the configuration refuses less than 8), 12 for any account holding a stored role wherever it sets its password, and a forced change at the next sign-in for such an account whose password is shorter. At most 128 characters, counted as characters. A blocklist, compared in lower case: the NCSC list of 100,000 filtered to the 46,528 entries of 8 characters and more (`data/NOTICE`), which is all of it that a minimum of 8 can meet. | `policy.rs:11-71`, `config.rs` (minimums 8 to 128), `oidc/flows.rs` (`after_password`) | The forced change follows a password sign-in, a Google sign-in, or a session signed in within the last 10 minutes; an older session must sign in again first (test `an_old_session_cannot_set_a_due_password_without_the_current_one`). |
| Imported bcrypt hashes | bcrypt hashes imported from a product are verified with the `bcrypt` crate and replaced with Argon2id at the next successful sign-in. | `crypto/password.rs:54-58` | **Accepted** (until every imported hash is replaced): `bcrypt::verify` (0.19) silently truncates at 72 bytes, so a longer password matches any password with the same first 72 bytes until the hash is replaced. Cost 12 takes a different time from the Argon2id dummy hash, so timing tells which addresses have an imported account that has not signed in since the import. |
| Learning who has an account | Sign-in: the same 401 and the dummy hash. Reset and verification requests always answer 202. Sign-up with a taken address answers like a new one, and the owner gets an "account exists" mail (at most 3 in 15 minutes per address). Sign-up leaves the flow in the same state for both, waiting for a confirmation (the taken address's flow bound to no account, so nothing completes it), with the same address shown; `resume` answers alike. An address change is counted first and answers 202 for a taken address too, sending no link. | `oidc/flows.rs:241-256`, `oidc/account.rs` (`signup`, `waiting_for`), `oidc/flows.rs` (`resume`), `me.rs` (`change_email`); tests `signing_up_confirming_and_signing_in`, `address_changes_hide_taken_addresses_and_end_with_a_new_password` | **Accepted:** a reset request for an unknown address returns before any write (`oidc/account.rs`, `request_reset`), a measurable difference of a few database writes. Without SMTP, sign-up signs a new address in at once and so tells taken from new (`oidc/account.rs:146-166`); the code says so. The bcrypt timing above. |
| Locking someone else out | Known browsers: the `__Host-pid_dev` cookie (HMAC with `PID_TOKEN_PEPPER` in the database) gives a browser that signed in to the account before its own counter, which strangers cannot fill. | `oidc/flows.rs:226-239`, `oidc/mod.rs:614-619` | Someone on a new browser is locked out with everyone else. Everyone behind one IPv4 address shares its limit. Behind a proxy that is not in `PID_TRUSTED_PROXIES`, every client has the proxy's address, so 30 wrong passwords lock sign-in for everybody for 15 minutes; the Helm chart refuses to render an Ingress without `trustedProxies`. Compose and other installations must set it themselves (runbook). |
| A forged client address | `X-Forwarded-For` is believed only from addresses in `PID_TRUSTED_PROXIES`, read from the right, up to the first address that is not a trusted proxy. | `net.rs:53-100`, `oidc/mod.rs:255-268` | The keyword `private_ranges` trusts every private address: a workload inside the network that reaches the public port directly can claim any address and escape the per-address limits. List the proxies' own addresses. |
| Reset, verification and address-change links | 32 random bytes, stored as SHA-256, used once (deleted on use), bound to the address they were sent to, the newest replacing older ones; reset 60 minutes, verification 24 hours. A reset proves the address, ends every session and clears the account's lock. Links are built from the realm's issuer, never from the Host header. Mail parameters, links included, are sealed under the key-encryption key, bound to the recipient, until sent. The pages take the token out of the address bar at once and send no referrer. Address changes still pending end with any sign-out everywhere, a reset and a password change, and a link is used only by the request that takes it (test `address_changes_hide_taken_addresses_and_end_with_a_new_password`). | `store/accounts.rs:166-226`, `oidc/account.rs:299-349`, `mail.rs:363-376`, `ui/src/lib/page.ts:15-22`, `ui.rs:168-170`, `oidc/logout.rs` (`end_all`), `me.rs` (`confirm_change`) | **Accepted** (left to the proxy, which this repository does not ship): the GET of `/reset-password?token=…`, `/verify-email?token=…` and `/invite?token=…` reaches the proxy with the token in the request line. pacod-id logs no request lines; each installation's proxy must keep query strings out of its access log or strip `code`, `state`, `token`, `id_token_hint` and `login_hint` (runbook, section 8). The mail relay and the mailbox see every link. The invitation page keeps its token in the tab's `sessionStorage`. |
| An unnoticed sign-in | A "new sign-in" mail when an account signs in from a browser it has not used while it has used others; a "password changed" mail after every change or reset. Changing the password ends the other sessions, a reset or an admin-set password all of them. | `oidc/mod.rs:614-626`, `me.rs:260-286`, `oidc/account.rs:328-339`, `admin/accounts.rs:256-270` | No mail for an account's first browser. A stolen device cookie avoids the mail. |

### Tokens and keys

| Threat | Control as built | Where in the code | Remaining risk |
|---|---|---|---|
| Theft of signing keys | RS256 keys of 2048 bits (generated with the `rsa` crate from the OS random source), ES256 only while a client uses it. Each private key is sealed with its own AES-256-GCM data key, wrapped by `PID_KEK`, with the key id as associated data. Rotation every 90 days, the next key published 24 hours ahead, retired keys published for 49 hours more. Emergency rotation with `pacod-id keys rotate --revoke <kid>`. `pacod-id rewrap` refuses while a running instance has another key-encryption key. The key-encryption key comes from the environment, `PID_KEK_FILE` or `PID_SECRETS_DIR`, never from the database. | `crypto/envelope.rs:97-133`, `keys.rs:21-27`, `:54-95`, `:152-219`, `main.rs:162-172`, `app.rs:236-245`, `bootstrap.rs:79-140` | A stolen key-encryption key plus a database dump yields every signing key. Keeping the key-encryption key out of database backups is the operator's job: pacod-id ships no backup script (`deploy/` holds compose, docker and helm only). An emergency rotation signs with the new key at once, without the 24 hours of publication; product libraries fetch the JWKS again on the unknown `kid` (at most every 30 seconds). `rewrap` moves the signing keys only, not the other sealed values (refresh successors, mail parameters). |
| Forged or confused tokens | One small JOSE crate on ring: only RS256 and ES256; `none`, unknown algorithms and `crit` are refused; the key's algorithm must match the header's; RSA keys of 2048 to 8192 bits; tokens of at most 16 KiB. `typ` separates access tokens (`at+jwt`), id_tokens (`JWT`) and logout tokens (`logout+jwt`), and every check looks at it. | `crates/pacod-id-jose/src/lib.rs:21`, `:111-125`, `:275-328`, `oidc/exchange.rs:71` | – |
| A stolen access token | 5 minutes by default (120 to 900 seconds per client), `aud` the client's product, a `sid` that products refuse once the feed carries its revocation. | `oidc/mod.rs:336-373`, `config.rs:368-370` | Valid until it expires at any product that does not follow the feed. Not sender-constrained (DPoP later). It carries `email` and `name` when those scopes were granted. |
| A token used at the wrong product | Product libraries check signature, `typ` `at+jwt`, issuer, audience, expiry with 30 seconds of leeway, the subject kind, and a `sid` for persons. Client-credentials and exchanged tokens carry `aud` `pacod-id` and are refused by products. | `crates/pacod-id-client/src/verifier.rs:104-153`, `python/src/pacod_id/verify.py:87-128`, `packages/client/src/tokens.ts:63-93` | Tokens of a delegated grant carry the product's audience and an `act` claim; the libraries accept them as the person and report the actor, and a product must look at it if it matters. |
| A stolen refresh token | `pidr_<grant>_<43 base62>`, stored as SHA-256, rotated on every use. A repeat of the token just rotated gets the same successor for 30 seconds (two tabs at once). An older token revokes every SSO session and grant of the account, writes `session.revoked` for each, queues back-channel logouts and mails the owner. A browser's grant ends with its SSO session; a native app's after 7 idle days, 365 at most. | `oidc/token.rs:152-249`, `oidc/mod.rs:35`, `:443-481`, `store/oidc.rs:386-410`, `:488-516` | The grace window: a thief who presents the stolen token within 30 seconds of the owner's refresh (or the owner within 30 seconds of the thief's) gets the same successor, and nothing is noticed until one of them later presents a token the other has rotated away. A thief who refreshes first holds the session until the owner refreshes. Anyone who holds a superseded token of the grant (an old copy of a BFF's store, a log) can sign the owner out everywhere: that is reuse. A token naming the grant with a secret it never had is only refused (every superseded hash is kept in `refresh_spent`; test `only_a_known_earlier_refresh_token_counts_as_reuse`), so the grant id alone, which logs may show, signs nobody out. The sealed successor is wiped by the maintenance job within a minute of its window, and one that cannot be opened (a key-encryption key rotated without `PID_KEK_OLD`) is refused like a late repeat. **Accepted:** the grace window, as above, so that two tabs refreshing at once are not taken for reuse. |
| Stolen or replayed authorization codes | 32 random bytes, stored as SHA-256, 60 seconds, used once; a second use by the same client revokes the grant the first produced, while another client's attempt is only refused (test `another_clients_code_neither_redeems_nor_revokes`). Bound to the client, the redirect URI and the PKCE challenge, which is checked on every exchange. | `oidc/token.rs` (`code_grant`), `store/oidc.rs:277-302` | **Accepted:** two takes of one code that start their transactions in the same microsecond would both see it unused; the PKCE verifier is needed for either. The access token a code produced is a JWT and stays valid until it expires (5 minutes); only the refresh token is revoked (the conformance suite warns of this). |
| Stolen or guessed service tokens | `pidt_<id>_<secret>` with 256 bits of secret, stored as HMAC-SHA256 with `PID_TOKEN_PEPPER` (16 bytes or more), shown once; the lookup skips revoked and expired tokens and disabled service accounts. The gateway's imported `pgwa_` tokens are checked under the gateway's pepper from `PID_LEGACY_PEPPERS`, and only under that one. | `crypto/tokens.rs:38-49`, `crypto/mod.rs:40-74`, `store/service.rs:21-49`, `oidc/exchange.rs:40-65` | The gateway's pepper lives in two services' configuration. The libraries' introspection cache keeps a revoked token working until `credential.revoked` reaches them. A service account's exchanged JWT is reported inactive as soon as the account is disabled or deleted. **Accepted:** any product's service may introspect any token and learn its subject, client and scope (`oidc/userinfo.rs`, `introspect`); services are trusted with what their callers send them. |
| Guessing client secrets | At least 32 characters (or 32 random bytes from `pacod-id clients secret`), stored as HMAC with the pepper, compared in constant time. | `oidc/mod.rs:167-204`, `bootstrap.rs:164-174`, `main.rs:144-160` | No limit on failed client authentication at the token endpoint, which the public listener serves too; 32 characters make guessing hopeless. |
| A product exchanging tokens it should not | Token exchange for service clients only. The subject token must be an access token pacod-id signed (`typ` `at+jwt`, a known issuer, unexpired) for the service's own `audience`, with a live session of the same account and an active account; or a live service token. The result lives 60 seconds, has `aud` `pacod-id` and `act.sub` the service. A delegated grant is bound to the session and ends after 12 hours at the latest. The configuration refuses a client that may exchange tokens without an `audience`, and the exchange refuses to run without one. | `oidc/exchange.rs` (`exchange`), `config.rs` (service clients); test `mistakes_are_refused_with_the_place` | **Accepted:** service tokens are tied to no product: any service may exchange any `pidt_` or `pgwa_` it holds. Within that, a compromised product acts for everyone who sends it a token, within their rights. |

### Browser flows

| Threat | Control as built | Where in the code | Remaining risk |
|---|---|---|---|
| Login CSRF and mixed-up flows | PKCE with S256 for every client, confidential and public; `state` returned; `nonce` in the id_token; `iss` (RFC 9207) on every redirect, errors included. ### Sessions and logout

| Threat | Control as built | Where in the code | Remaining risk |
|---|---|---|---|
| Forced sign-out | `/oidc/logout` ends the browser's session at once only with an `id_token_hint` that pacod-id signed for this realm (`typ` `JWT`) and that names the session or its account; otherwise a confirmation page asks first. | `oidc/logout.rs:161-302` | **Accepted:** when the browser has no session, a GET with any id_token of the person, of any age while its key is still published, ends the session it names (`oidc/logout.rs:290-299`). | Clients missing a sign-out | Back-channel logout through a PostgreSQL outbox: the logout token is signed at each attempt (new `iat`, `jti`, two-minute expiry, `typ` `logout+jwt`), posted with the issuer as `Origin`, without following redirects, retried with backoff for 10 minutes. ## Not covered

- The host, the cluster, the database server and their administrators; anyone who holds the
  key-encryption key and a database dump.
- A compromised product service acting within its product: it acts for everyone who sends it a
  token, within their rights; it can end any session and introspect any token; it reads its
  product's feed.
- Phishing of a password, and malware in the person's browser or device: MFA and passkeys come
  in phase 4.
- Denial of service beyond the throttles: there is no general rate limit, none on the token
  endpoint, on long polls per service or on the hashing queue's length.
- The mailbox: whoever reads a person's mail can reset their password and confirm addresses.
- Google's own account security, and what Google reports as verified.
- TLS: the proxy terminates it at the edge; the internal listener speaks plain HTTP.
- Products' own handling of tokens, sessions, the feed and their staleness rules; each
  product's threat model covers them.
- Secrets that people put into display names, labels or other free text.
